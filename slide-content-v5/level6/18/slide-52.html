Last year, Omar's family spent Ramadan in Cairo, where his mother grew up. He expected it to be the same as Riyadh. It was not. Cairo's Ramadan is louder, more colorful and more crowded, and the streets never sleep. Every lane has fanous lanterns, and long tables appear on the sidewalk where anyone, rich or poor, can eat iftar for free.

Riyadh's Ramadan, Omar realized, is more traditional in a different way. The streets are quieter, the neighbors send dishes to each other's doors at sunset, and the majlis is more important than the street. Families gather at home, and the night belongs to the mosque and the quiet cafés.

Some things were exactly the same. Both cities break the fast with dates first. In both, the mosques are full for Taraweeh, although in Cairo the streets outside are far louder. Both become kinder: in Cairo his uncle gave food to strangers every night, and in Riyadh his grandmother cooks for the whole building.

"Which Ramadan is better?" Sara asked when he came back. Omar thought about the lanterns and the quiet streets, the crowded tables and the quiet majlis. "Cairo is the most colorful place I know," he said. "Riyadh is the most peaceful. But hospitality is the same in both. That is the heritage, and it does not change with the city."
