In Ramadan, Hamad's day turns upside down. He usually wakes up at seven, but in Ramadan he wakes up at four for suhoor. His mother makes eggs and warm bread, and his grandmother always says, "Eat slowly, it is a long day."

School starts late, so Hamad rarely feels tired in class. In the afternoon, he sometimes sleeps, and occasionally he reads. He never plays football before iftar. "Football at four o'clock in Ramadan? That is a bad habit," his father says.

At sunset, the whole family sits together. First, dates and water. Then soup, then everything else. Twice a week, his uncle's family comes, and the table is twice as long. After iftar, Hamad walks to the mosque with his father for taraweeh. The streets are full of people, lights and the smell of coffee.

At night, the city wakes up. Shops open, cousins visit, and kids play in the street until midnight. Hamad constantly checks the time, because suhoor comes quickly. Ramadan is tiring, but he loves it. "It is one month," he says, "but we talk about it all year."
