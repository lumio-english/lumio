Where English Words Come From

من أين جاءت الكلمات الإنجليزية؟
Big idea: English is a Germanic language that borrowed thousands of words from French, Latin, Greek and many others, including Arabic; that is why it often has two words for one idea, an everyday one and a formal one.
الإنجليزية لغة جرمانية استعارت آلاف الكلمات من الفرنسية واللاتينية واليونانية وغيرها، ومنها العربية؛ ولهذا كثيراً ما نجد فيها كلمتين لفكرة واحدة: كلمة يومية وكلمة رسمية.

Linguistics Corner — Family trees and layers: the life story of English · شجرة العائلة والطبقات: قصة حياة الإنجليزية

Languages have families. English belongs to the West Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, with German, Dutch and Frisian. Its distant cousins include French, Spanish, Russian, Hindi and even Persian. Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch of the Afro-Asiatic family, with Hebrew, Aramaic, Amharic and Maltese.للغات عائلات. الإنجليزية تنتمي إلى الفرع الجرماني الغربي من العائلة الهندية الأوروبية، مع الألمانية والهولندية والفريزية. ومن أبناء عمومتها البعيدين: الفرنسية والإسبانية والروسية والهندية بل والفارسية. أما العربية فتنتمي إلى الفرع السامي من العائلة الأفريقية الآسيوية، مع العبرية والآرامية والأمهرية والمالطية.
Surprise: Persian is written in Arabic letters and has many Arabic words, but it is related to English, not to Arabic! Compare Persian pedar, mādar, barādar with English father, mother, brother.مفاجأة: الفارسية تُكتب بالحروف العربية وفيها كلمات عربية كثيرة، لكنها قريبة من الإنجليزية لا من العربية! قارن 'پدر، مادر، برادر' بـ father, mother, brother.
English grew in layers. About 1,500 years ago the Angles and Saxons brought Old English (house, water, eat, good). Viking settlers added Old Norse words (sky, egg, knife, they, trust; window = 'wind-eye'). After the Norman Conquest of 1066, French was the language of the rulers for about 300 years, and thousands of French words came in (court, government, manners, curfew). In the Renaissance, scholars added Latin and Greek words (independent, technology).نمت الإنجليزية على طبقات. قبل نحو 1500 سنة جلب الأنجلز والساكسون الإنجليزية القديمة (house, water, eat, good). ثم أضاف مستوطنو الفايكنج كلمات من النوردية القديمة (sky, egg, knife, they, trust؛ و window تعني 'عين الريح'). وبعد الغزو النورماندي سنة 1066 صارت الفرنسية لغة الحكّام نحو 300 سنة، فدخلت آلاف الكلمات الفرنسية (court, government, manners, curfew). وفي عصر النهضة أضاف العلماء كلمات لاتينية ويونانية (independent, technology).
Counting dictionary words, only about a quarter of English vocabulary is Germanic; most of the rest is French and Latin. But in real speech the picture flips: almost all of the 100 most frequent English words (the, is, and, have, you, it) are Germanic.إذا عددنا كلمات القاموس فإن نحو ربع المفردات الإنجليزية فقط جرماني، ومعظم الباقي فرنسي ولاتيني. لكن في الكلام الحقيقي تنقلب الصورة: تقريباً كل الكلمات المئة الأكثر استخداماً (the, is, and, have, you, it) جرمانية.
Arabic words reached English mostly in the Middle Ages and later, usually indirectly: through Latin translations of Arabic science (made in places like Toledo in Spain), through Spanish (al-Andalus), through Italian merchants (Venice, Genoa) and through Turkish. Arabic itself had borrowed some of them first (sugar from Persian, which took it from Sanskrit).وصلت الكلمات العربية إلى الإنجليزية غالباً في العصور الوسطى وما بعدها، وبطريق غير مباشر في الأغلب: عبر الترجمات اللاتينية للعلوم العربية (في مدن مثل طليطلة في إسبانيا)، وعبر الإسبانية (الأندلس)، وعبر التجار الإيطاليين (البندقية وجنوة)، وعبر التركية. وبعض هذه الكلمات استعارته العربية نفسها قبل ذلك (سكر من الفارسية التي أخذتها من السنسكريتية).

1. Warm-up: Hidden Arabic

0–5 min
إحماء: العربية المختبئة
Method: Listening hook with a mini-story (noticing)
Purpose: Make students notice that English already contains Arabic, and raise the question 'how did it get there?'.
Teacher says

“Listen to a short story. Raise your hand every time you hear a word that sounds a bit Arabic to you.”

“'On Saturday morning, Hamad sat on the sofa with a cup of coffee with lots of sugar. He was reading a magazine about a safari in Kenya, where he saw a giraffe. Then he opened his algebra homework: question one was zero out of ten!'”

“How many did you find? Write them in the chat. Today we are word detectives: where do English words come from, and how did Arabic words travel to London?”

  • استمعوا للقصة وارفعوا أيديكم كلما سمعتم كلمة تشبه العربية.
  • اليوم نحن محققو كلمات: من أين جاءت الكلمات الإنجليزية؟ وكيف سافرت الكلمات العربية إلى لندن؟
Students do
  • Listen twice and raise a hand at 'Arabic-sounding' words.
  • Write the words in the chat (sofa, coffee, sugar, magazine, safari, giraffe, algebra, zero).
  • Guess the Arabic word behind each one.
Tips:
  • Do not confirm all answers yet; keep the suspense for the detective stage.
  • If students suggest a wrong word (e.g. 'Saturday'), praise the guess and save it: Saturday is from Latin Saturn, a great discussion point later.

2. Linguistics Corner: The Family Tree of Languages

5–15 min
ركن اللغويات: شجرة عائلة اللغات
Method: Visual discovery (family tree) + evidence from basic words
Purpose: Understand language families, place English and Arabic in different families, and learn how linguists prove two languages are related.
Teacher says

“Languages have families, like people. How do linguists know two languages are related? They compare basic words, words every family has: mother, father, two, three.”

“Look: English mother, German Mutter, Dutch moeder. English three, German drei, Dutch drie. They are clearly brothers: the Germanic branch.”

“Now Arabic: ثلاثة, Hebrew shalosh. أم, Hebrew em. Another family: Semitic.”

“Here is the big surprise. Persian: pedar, mādar, barādar. Father, mother, brother! Persian is written in Arabic letters, but it is English's distant cousin, not Arabic's.”

“So English and Arabic are not related. When English and Arabic words look alike, it is usually a borrowing: a word that travelled.”

  • للغات عائلات مثل البشر. يقارن اللغويون الكلمات الأساسية: أم، أب، اثنان، ثلاثة.
  • مفاجأة: الفارسية تُكتب بحروف عربية لكنها قريبة من الإنجليزية!
  • الإنجليزية والعربية ليستا من عائلة واحدة؛ الكلمات المتشابهة بينهما غالباً مسافرة (مستعارة).
Students do
  • Compare number and family words across 4 languages on the slide and circle the similar sounds.
  • Place 10 languages on the family tree (exercise).
  • Explain in one sentence why Persian is a surprise.
Exercise · family-tree-sort — Put each language on the right branch of the tree.
ضع كل لغة على الفرع الصحيح من الشجرة.
  1. German → Indo-European - Germanic (English's sister)
  2. Dutch → Indo-European - Germanic
  3. French → Indo-European - Romance (from Latin)
  4. Spanish → Indo-European - Romance (from Latin)
  5. Hindi → Indo-European - Indo-Iranian
  6. Persian (Farsi) → Indo-European - Indo-Iranian (written in Arabic script)
  7. Hebrew → Afro-Asiatic - Semitic (Arabic's sister)
  8. Amharic (Ethiopia) → Afro-Asiatic - Semitic
  9. Maltese → Afro-Asiatic - Semitic (grew from Arabic, written in Latin letters)
  10. Turkish → Turkic family (neither Indo-European nor Semitic)
Tips:
  • Maltese and Turkish are the 'trick' items: Maltese looks European but is Semitic; Turkish was once written in Arabic letters but belongs to a different family.
  • Keep it visual and quick; the goal is the concept of families, not memorizing branches.

3. The Word-Origin Timeline: Layers of English

15–23 min
خط زمن أصول الكلمات: طبقات الإنجليزية
Method: Storytelling with a timeline + matching (retrieval)
Purpose: Show that English vocabulary arrived in layers, each with a story, using words from Lessons 9-12.
Teacher says

“English is like a cake with layers. Layer 1, around the year 450: the Angles and Saxons. They give us everyday words: house, water, eat, good, kind, worth.”

“Layer 2, from about 800: the Vikings. Words like sky, egg, knife, they, and trust from Lesson 10! window comes from 'wind-eye': a hole for the wind.”

“Layer 3, 1066: the Normans from France win the Battle of Hastings. For about 300 years, the rulers speak French. Words like court, government, manners and curfew. Curfew comes from French 'cover fire': at night, people had to cover their fires.”

“Layer 4, the Renaissance: scholars add Latin and Greek: independent, technology, solution.”

“Layer 5, today: English borrows from everywhere: emoji and tsunami from Japanese, shampoo from Hindi. And travel? From French travailler, 'to work hard'. Travelling used to be really hard work!”

  • الإنجليزية مثل كعكة من طبقات.
  • curfew أصلها فرنسي ومعناها 'غطِّ النار': في الليل كان على الناس أن يغطوا نيرانهم.
  • travel من الفرنسية travailler أي 'يعمل بجهد': كان السفر عملاً شاقاً حقاً!
Students do
  • Follow the timeline and repeat one fun fact per layer.
  • Drag each word to its layer on the timeline slide.
  • Tell a partner the story of one word (curfew, window or travel).
Exercise · timeline-match — Put each word on the right layer of the timeline.
ضع كل كلمة على الطبقة الصحيحة في خط الزمن.
  1. water → Old English (Anglo-Saxon)
  2. sky → Old Norse (Vikings)
  3. trust → Old Norse (Vikings)
  4. curfew → Norman French (after 1066)
  5. manners → Norman French (after 1066), from Latin manus 'hand'
  6. technology → Greek (1600s)
  7. independent → Latin (1600s)
  8. emoji → Japanese (modern global English)
Tips:
  • Dates are approximate; give round numbers and focus on the order of the layers.
  • Link to Lesson 10 vocabulary: curfew, manners and trust come from three different layers.

4. Arabic Bridge: The Etymology Detective Map

23–35 min
جسر العربية: خريطة محقق أصول الكلمات
Method: Detective game in teams + map annotation (multimodal)
Purpose: Trace English words that came through Arabic, find their Arabic source and draw the route they travelled.
Teacher says

“Detectives, here is your case file. Each card has an English word. Find the Arabic word behind it and draw its route on the map.”

“Example: algebra. Arabic: الجبر, from the title of a 9th-century book by al-Khwarizmi in Baghdad. Route: Baghdad, then Latin translators in Spain, then Europe, then English. Fun fact: in old Spanish, an 'algebrista' was a bone-setter, because الجبر also means setting broken bones!”

“Careful: many words travelled through other languages first: Italian, Spanish, Turkish, French. And some words were not Arabic at the start: sugar came into Arabic from Persian, and earlier from Sanskrit. So we say 'came into English through Arabic'.”

“Notice the al-: algebra, algorithm, alcohol. That is the Arabic 'ال' travelling inside the word!”

  • أيها المحققون: لكل بطاقة كلمة إنجليزية، ابحثوا عن الكلمة العربية خلفها وارسموا طريقها على الخريطة.
  • كثير من الكلمات مرّت بلغات أخرى أولاً: الإيطالية والإسبانية والتركية والفرنسية.
  • لاحظوا 'ال' التعريف داخل الكلمات: algebra, alcohol!
Students do
  • In two teams, solve 12 word cards: Arabic source + route.
  • Draw the route arrows on the shared map with the annotation tool.
  • Score: 1 point for the Arabic word, 1 point for the route; the teacher reveals the answers.
  • Choose the most surprising word and say why.
Exercise · etymology-detective — Find the Arabic word and the route into English.
اكتشف الكلمة العربية والطريق الذي سلكته إلى الإنجليزية.
  1. sugar → سكر - via Medieval Latin and French (Arabic took it from Persian shakar, originally Sanskrit)
  2. coffee → قهوة - via Turkish kahve and Italian caffè / Dutch koffie
  3. cotton → قطن - via Italian cotone and Old French coton
  4. algebra → الجبر - from al-Khwarizmi's book title, via Medieval Latin
  5. algorithm → the name الخوارزمي (al-Khwarizmi) - via Medieval Latin algorismus
  6. zero → صفر - via Medieval Latin and Italian (cipher also comes from صفر)
  7. giraffe → زرافة - via Italian giraffa
  8. sofa → صُفّة (a raised bench with cushions) - via Turkish and French
  9. magazine → مخازن (storehouses) - via Italian magazzino and French magasin
  10. admiral → أمير (as in أمير البحر) - via Medieval Latin and Old French amiral
  11. alcohol → الكحل (a fine powder for the eyes) - via Medieval Latin; the meaning changed completely
  12. safari → سفر - via Swahili safari (East Africa)
Tips:
  • Give weaker teams the Arabic words in a jumbled list to match instead of finding them.
  • Be honest about uncertainty: etymologists sometimes disagree about exact routes; say 'probably via...' when needed.
  • Extra words for fast teams: lemon (ليمون, from Persian), candy (قند, ultimately Persian/Sanskrit), syrup (شراب), arsenal (دار الصناعة).

5. Two Words for One Idea: Everyday vs Formal

35–45 min
كلمتان لفكرة واحدة: يومية ورسمية
Method: Guided discovery of synonym layers + register rewriting
Purpose: Understand why English has Germanic everyday words and French/Latin formal words, and choose the right one for the situation.
Teacher says

“After 1066, the farmers spoke English and the rulers spoke French. A famous story: the animal in the field kept its English name, but the meat on the rich man's table got a French name. cow - beef, sheep - mutton, calf - veal, deer - venison.”

“The real history is a bit more complicated, but the pattern is real: English often has an everyday Germanic word and a formal French or Latin word. buy - purchase, help - assist, ask - inquire, start - commence, need - require.”

“Sometimes there are three! kingly (Germanic), royal (French), regal (Latin). ask, question, interrogate.”

“Which one do we use? With friends: the short Germanic word. In signs, emails and official texts: the longer French or Latin word.”

  • بعد 1066 تكلم الفلاحون الإنجليزية والحكّام الفرنسية: الحيوان في الحقل اسمه إنجليزي، واللحم على مائدة الغني اسمه فرنسي.
  • مع الأصدقاء: الكلمة الجرمانية القصيرة. في اللافتات والرسائل الرسمية: الكلمة الفرنسية أو اللاتينية الأطول.
Students do
  • Match everyday words with their formal partners.
  • Rewrite a formal mall notice in everyday English for a friend, then a friend's message in formal English for an email.
  • Discuss: which word sounds friendlier? which sounds more serious?
Exercise · register-switch — Give the everyday word for the formal one, or the formal word for the everyday one.
اكتب الكلمة اليومية مقابل الرسمية، أو الرسمية مقابل اليومية.
  1. purchase (formal) -> everyday? → buy
  2. assist (formal) -> everyday? → help
  3. inquire (formal) -> everyday? → ask
  4. commence (formal) -> everyday? → start / begin
  5. need (everyday) -> formal? → require
  6. enough (everyday) -> formal? → sufficient
  7. cow (animal) -> meat? → beef
  8. Formal notice: 'Customers who wish to purchase tickets should inquire at the desk.' -> everyday → If you want to buy tickets, ask at the desk.
  9. Everyday: 'Can you help me? I need more time.' -> formal email → Could you assist me? I require more time.
Tips:
  • Formal is not 'better': using purchase with friends sounds strange or funny. Good English means choosing the right register.
  • Connect to Arabic: فصحى vs عامية is a similar choice, but English mixes the two layers inside one language every day.

6. Word Biographies and Hybrid Hunters

45–53 min
سِيَر الكلمات وصيادو الكلمات الهجينة
Method: Creative mini-presentation + peer feedback
Purpose: Use the new knowledge to tell the life story of one word and find words built from parts of different languages.
Teacher says

“Some words are hybrids: their parts come from different languages. affordable = afford (Old English) + -able (French and Latin). useful = use (French) + -ful (Old English). body language = body (Old English) + language (French, from Latin lingua, 'tongue').”

“Now choose one word from today or from Lessons 9-12 and write its 'passport': Born in... / Arrived in English... / Route... / Old meaning... / Today's meaning...”

“You have 3 minutes to prepare and 30 seconds to present. Speak clearly and confidently, like a museum guide!”

  • بعض الكلمات هجينة: أجزاؤها من لغات مختلفة.
  • اختر كلمة واكتب 'جواز سفرها': مكان الولادة، وقت الوصول، الطريق، المعنى القديم، المعنى اليوم.
  • قدّمها خلال 30 ثانية بوضوح وثقة كمرشد في متحف!
Students do
  • Split 6 words into parts and name the origin of each part.
  • Write a word passport (5 lines).
  • Present it in 30 seconds; peers give one 'star' (something good) and one 'wish' (something to improve).
Exercise · split-the-hybrid — Split the word into parts and give the origin of each part.
قسّم الكلمة إلى أجزاء واذكر أصل كل جزء.
  1. affordable → afford (Old English) + -able (French/Latin)
  2. useful → use (French) + -ful (Old English)
  3. clearly → clear (French) + -ly (Old English)
  4. body language → body (Old English) + language (French, from Latin lingua)
  5. unreliable → un- (Old English) + rely (French) + -able (French/Latin)
  6. kindly → kind (Old English) + -ly (Old English): 100% Germanic
Tips:
  • Suggested passport words: curfew, travel, window, manners, technology, coffee, algebra, sofa.
  • Use a simple peer rubric: clear voice, one fact, one route, eye contact.

7. Exit Ticket, Did You Know? and Goodbye

53–60 min
بطاقة الخروج وهل تعلم؟ والوداع
Method: Formative check + curiosity close
Purpose: Check the key ideas (families, layers, Arabic loanwords, register) and send students home with a fact to share.
Teacher says

“Exit ticket: five quick questions. Three minutes.”

“Did you know? The word 'check' in chess comes from Persian shāh, 'king', and 'checkmate' from shāh māt, which reached Europe through Arabic. When you say 'checkmate', you are speaking Persian through Arabic!”

“Your mission: find three English words in a shop, a menu or an app that came into English through Arabic. Bring them to the next class.”

  • هل تعلم؟ كلمة check في الشطرنج من الفارسية 'شاه' أي الملك، و checkmate من 'شاه مات' التي وصلت أوروبا عبر العربية!
  • مهمتك: ابحث عن ثلاث كلمات إنجليزية في متجر أو قائمة طعام أو تطبيق دخلت الإنجليزية عن طريق العربية.
Students do
  • Complete the 5 exit-ticket items.
  • Say one word and its origin as their goodbye ('Goodbye from algebra, born in Baghdad!').
Tips:
  • This class adds no topic vocabulary to test; assess the understanding of families, layers and register.
  • Collect the mission words for a 'Lumio Word Museum' slide in Bonus Class 4 or Lesson 17.

Exit ticket

Exercise · exit ticket — Answer each question.
أجب عن كل سؤال.
  1. Is English a Germanic or a Semitic language? → Germanic (Indo-European)
  2. Which event in 1066 brought thousands of French words into English? → The Norman Conquest
  3. Give the Arabic word behind 'cotton'. → قطن
  4. Everyday word for 'purchase'? → buy
  5. True or false: Persian and English are in the same language family. → True (both Indo-European)

Homework

Word Museum: make a mini-museum of 5 English words that came into English through Arabic (or other languages). For each word, write its 'passport' (origin, route, old and new meaning) and draw its route on a small map.

متحف الكلمات: اصنع متحفاً صغيراً لخمس كلمات إنجليزية دخلت الإنجليزية عن طريق العربية (أو لغات أخرى). اكتب لكل كلمة 'جواز سفرها' (الأصل، الطريق، المعنى القديم والجديد) وارسم طريقها على خريطة صغيرة.

  • Write 5 word passports and draw one route map.
  • Find 3 pairs of everyday/formal words in a real sign, email or website (buy/purchase type).
  • Ask a family member which English words they use in Arabic every day (موبايل، باص...) and list 5.

Support

  • Give the Arabic words for the detective game as a matching list.
  • Use only three timeline layers: Old English, French (1066), Latin/Greek.
  • Provide the word passport as a fill-in template with sentence starters.

Challenge

  • Use an online etymology dictionary to trace two Lesson 9-12 words (e.g. efficient, achieve) and present their old meanings.
  • Find a triplet like kingly / royal / regal (Germanic / French / Latin): e.g. rise / mount / ascend.
  • Explain why a word's route matters: why does 'coffee' have a different vowel from قهوة? (It changed in Turkish and Italian.)

Success criteria

  • Places at least 8 of 10 languages on the correct branch of the family tree.
  • Explains the Norman Conquest layer and gives at least two French-origin words.
  • Finds the Arabic source of at least 8 of 12 detective words and describes at least three routes.
  • Switches correctly between everyday and formal words in at least 7 of 9 items.

Watch for

  • Thinking English and Arabic are related because of loanwords
  • Using formal words (purchase, commence, inquire) in casual speech
  • Treating magazine as 'store' (false friend)
  • Saying every Arabic-looking word came directly from Arabic (many came via Persian, Turkish, Italian or Spanish)

Teacher notes

  • Etymologies in this class follow standard dictionaries (Oxford, Merriam-Webster, Etymonline). Where routes are debated, say 'probably'.
  • The cow/beef story was made famous by the novelist Walter Scott (Ivanhoe); historians say the reality was more mixed, but the Germanic-everyday / French-formal pattern is well established.
  • The percentages (about a quarter Germanic in the dictionary; almost all of the 100 most common words Germanic) are approximate and depend on the word list used.
  • Avoid religion or national comparisons ('Arabic is better/older'); the message is that languages share and borrow, and Arabic science shaped European vocabulary.
  • Bonus Class 4 (The Art of Persuasion) will reuse the formal/everyday choice in speeches and the Greek origin of rhetoric (ethos, pathos, logos).
