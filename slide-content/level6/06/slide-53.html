Hamad types quickly. He texts quickly, he games quickly, and he does his homework quickly, sometimes too quickly. So his mother signed him up for a calligraphy course at the cultural center. "One hour a week," she said, "where you do something slowly."

The teacher, Ustadh Khalid, started writing forty years ago. On the first day he did not let the students write a single letter. They sat quietly and cut their reed pens carefully, at exactly the right angle. Then they mixed the ink patiently, and then they went home. Hamad was annoyed. "We did nothing," he told his father. "You prepared," his father said. "That is not nothing."

In week three, Hamad wrote his first alif. Badly. Ustadh Khalid looked at it thoughtfully and said, "Again, but more gently. The pen is not a sword." Hamad tried again, and again, and on the ninth try the line was straight. He stared at it for a long time.

Now, six months later, he writes his name in Thuluth, slowly and accurately, and it hangs above his desk. He still games loudly and texts quickly. But when he picks up the reed pen, something changes. He breathes deeply, and for one hour, the world moves at the speed of ink.
