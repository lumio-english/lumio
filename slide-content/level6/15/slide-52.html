Last week, Omar's class held its first real debate. The motion was simple: schools should ban phones. The teacher put a box on her desk, collected every phone, and locked the box. "Now," she said, "you have thirty minutes to argue about it."

Hamad's team argued for the ban. From his point of view, the real problem was attention: students check their phones every six minutes, and nobody can learn in six-minute pieces. He had evidence, a statistic from a study, and he spoke slowly so everyone could write it down. "A class without phones is quieter and more focused," he said. Noor's team disagreed. "Phones are not the problem," she said. "Boring lessons are a bigger problem, and they are more common than phones." Half the room laughed; the other half took notes.

Then came the counterarguments. Omar agreed partly with both sides. "A fair point," he said to Hamad, "but a ban does not teach us self-control. It only delays the problem until we leave school." The teacher wrote 'self-control' on the board and underlined it twice.

After the conclusions, the class voted, and Noor's team won by two votes. But the most interesting moment came after the bell. Nobody ran to the box. The students stayed, still arguing, still convincing each other. The phones waited quietly in the dark, and for once, nobody missed them.
